1. Integration: Failures Live at the Boundaries

Lumetriq's Boundaries

The example from class has four boundaries. Lumetriq has five, because there are two radio hops between the sensor and the app: the pods talk to each other over ESP-NOW, and only one of them, the gateway, talks to the phone over BLE (Week 5 architecture).

Lumetriq's five boundaries. When something fails, look for the first boundary where the data already arrives wrong.

BoundaryQuestionLog Set Up Before It Is Needed
B1 · Sensors → ESP32 (SPI)Is the reading reliable?Diagnostic mode: raw ADS1220 and ADXL375 signals with µs timestamps, through the Bulk characteristic
B2 · Follower → gateway (ESP-NOW)Does the event leave and arrive?Send, retry, and loss counters on each pod, carried in the STATUS message every 5 s
B3 · Gateway → app (BLE)Does the phone receive everything, with no duplicates?The last seq received per pod in SQLite vs. the last one sent by the pod
B4 · App → cloud → LLM (HTTPS)Is the recommendation usable?Raw input and output of every call in the Edge Function; the output is validated against a schema, and every figure is checked against the input JSON
B5 · App → userDoes the user see the right thing, on time?Every piece of data on screen shows its timestamp; an "offline, last data N s ago" state

How T4 Is Calculated, with Our Numbers

Assumptions (the team replaces them with the measured discharge):
  1,800 mAh 18650 cell (the prototype's); usable down to the
  3.40 V cutoff ≈ 1,500 mAh [Estimated]
  PDS target (PR-01): ≥ 3 h  →  maximum draw = 1,500 / 3 = 500 mA

PDS profile, gateway pod (the worst-case role):
  LED arc: 326 mA measured (cyan at 100%) × 40% brightness
           × 30% of the time on                              =  39 mA
  LEDs off: 18 × ≈ 0.6 mA                                    =  11 mA [Estimated]
  ESP32 with BLE + ESP-NOW + sensors: 133 mA at 3.3 V, going
  through the AMS1117 (5 V) and the MT3608 from 3.7 V at 85% efficiency:
           133 × 5 / (3.7 × 0.85)                            = 211 mA
  Average draw                                               = 261 mA

Battery life = 1,500 mAh / 261 mA = 5.7 h  →  complies (> 3 h)
With the batch cell (2,200 mAh, ≈ 1,830 mAh usable)          = 7.0 h

Takeaway: the ESP32 accounts for 81% of the draw, and the MT3608 + AMS1117 path wastes energy. If T4 falls short, the first lever is a low-dropout regulator running straight from the battery, not a bigger cell.

5. Unit Economics: CAC, LTV, Churn, and Payback

Prompt 3 with the Week 7 Numbers

Adaptation: the example from class charges a monthly fee and subsidizes the hardware. Lumetriq sells the package as a one-time payment with the subscription included, so (1) the customer is the gym, (2) the hardware is not subsidized, and the investment per customer is just the CAC, (3) the LTV comes from the initial purchase plus repeat purchases, and (4) "churn" is a gym that stops using the app and stops buying. Since the app keeps working without charging, the cloud and AI cost is subtracted over the customer's entire lifetime.

DATA CARRIED OVER FROM WEEK 7 (year 1)
  Price per package before VAT                                = $5,172.41
  Components + equipment per package (4,000 + 36)             = $4,036.00
  Cloud + AI per package per year (AI 54.84 + server 5,610/300) = $73.54
  Typical purchase: 3 packages per gym (Week 4)
  Advertising: 10% of the price = $600 per package
  The team's hourly rate (Week 7)                             = $195/h

ASSUMPTIONS (measured in the pilot)
  Close rate: 1 in 3 demos
  Each demo, including travel: 2.5 h
  Travel expenses and samples per closed gym: $300
  Annual churn (leaves the app and stops buying): 30%
  Repeat purchase: 1 package per year while the gym stays active

CALCULATION
  Average lifetime = 1 ÷ 0.30                                 = 3.33 years
  Service over the lifetime = 73.54 × 3.33                    = $245.13 per package
  Contribution per package = 5,172.41 − 4,036 − 245.13        = $891.28
  Packages per gym = 3 + (3.33 − 1)                           = 5.33
  LTV = 891.28 × 5.33                                         = $4,753
  CAC = 3 demos × 2.5 h × $195 + $300 + 3 × $600              = $3,562.50
  Investment per customer = CAC (the gym pays for the hardware) = $3,562.50
  LTV ÷ investment = 4,753 ÷ 3,562.50                         = 1.33
  Payback: the initial purchase leaves 3 × 891.28 = $2,673.84; the
  remaining $888.66 comes with the first repeat purchase (year 2) ≈ 12 months

Takeaway: payback lands right at the 12-month limit, but the LTV ÷ investment ratio of 1.33 is far from 3. With these assumptions, the model doesn't work. This is the information we want to have before manufacturing 300 packages.

8. The 3-Minute Pitch

Script (Prompt 5 with Our Material)

1. Impact and problem. Think of a boxing coach in Puebla, like the ones we interviewed. To train reflexes, he shouts, claps, and points at cones. While he's busy being the traffic light, he isn't correcting technique, and nobody takes a number home. Every student who gets bored and leaves costs the gym 850 pesos a month. The administrator we interviewed summed it up: today, everything is free. The problem isn't measuring: it's getting students to want to come back.

2. Solution and why us. Lumetriq is a set of pods that bolt to the wall. The light tells you where to punch, and every punch gives you reaction time and force. The group leaderboard carries over from week to week, so it's worth coming back. Cones don't keep score, and the light pods that already exist don't measure force or keep a leaderboard between classes. In the demo, you'll see it react live.

3. Market and model. In Mexico, there are 2,939 gyms and studios that could buy it, and 1,131 of them are within our reach. We sell 4-pod packages for 6,000 pesos, with the app included and no monthly fee. Three packages cost less than what two students pay in a year. With 800 packages in two years, the project is worth 1.4 million pesos today, and the investor gets back 2.1 times what they put in.

4. Traction. We interviewed four people from gyms, and the same thing came up in all four interviews: making training more fun. The prototype already has its circuit board and a measured light arc, and at that draw, one battery lasts more than three hours. We don't have pre-registrations yet. Our per-customer economics come to 1.3 against a target of 3, and we know what moves it: price and referrals between coaches.

5. Team. We are three engineering students at Ibero Puebla: hardware, app, and business. We designed, built, and calculated all of this, and we don't take a salary: we are partners.

6. The ask and close. We're asking for three boxing gyms in Puebla for a 30-day pilot, with one package each. We'll measure the only thing we're still missing: how many buy after trying it and how many recommend us. The coach doesn't need another timer. He needs his students to want to come back.

Five Vibe-Coding Mistakes with Hardware, and How We Avoid Them

  1. Letting the model invent the data format: every prompt includes the real JSON above.
  2. Testing only with perfect data: the simulator sends duplicate and out-of-order events, and dropouts.
  3. Letting the app run the routine: in Lumetriq, the gateway runs it; the prompt says "the app does not send stimuli."
  4. Showing old data as current: every screen shows the data's timestamp and the offline state.
  5. Putting the AI key in the app: it goes in the Edge Function (Week 5), never on the phone.

14. Reflection and Key Takeaways

This week forced us to see Lumetriq as a system rather than a pod. When we drew the boundaries, we realized we have five, not four, because the pods talk to each other before they talk to the phone, and that the most fragile point is the gateway, where ESP-NOW and Bluetooth share the same radio. That is when the rule of setting up logs before you need them clicked for us: if a punch gets lost during the demo, without the sequence number we wouldn't know where. The battery life calculation with our measured draw was satisfying, because it confirms the PDS's 3 hours with our own data, and it also showed that the ESP32, not the LEDs, uses almost all the energy.

The unit economics were the most uncomfortable part. Adapting the formulas to a one-time payment showed us that, with our assumptions, a gym brings in 1.33 times what it costs to acquire it, far from the reference value of 3, and that the strongest lever is price, not retention. We didn't adjust the numbers until they looked good; what we learned instead is what would have to be true (a gym paying $6,500, and half of the sales coming from referrals), and that is exactly what the pilot has to measure.

Writing the pitch with a word limit also taught us to say only what we can back up: several good lines had to stay marked as pending because we don't have the evidence yet. Going into the final two weeks, the priority is clear: get a punch to travel from the pod to the screen on its own, and put the product in front of real gym owners.
