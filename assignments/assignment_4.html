Assignment 4: Market, Value & Value Proposition

In this phase, we synthesized our user interviews to adjust our core hypotheses, mapped out the actionable market opportunity, evaluated the product with Bain's Elements of Value pyramid, sized the market (TAM/SAM/SOM) using the 4-pod package from the Week 7 business case, built a competitive map and a Blue Ocean strategy canvas, wrote our value proposition, and critically reviewed whether our AI-generated concept actually looks like a device that can take a punch. Generative AI (Claude and Perplexity) helped us structure the analysis; every figure was checked against sources and interviews.

1. Interview Conclusions & Hypothesis Adjustments

Original Hypotheses

  • H1 (Business Model): HIIT/functional studio owners would pay $1,500–2,000 MXN/month for the software (buying the pods at cost) because it automates circuit direction and increases retention. If false: the SaaS dies, and we get trapped in a hardware price war.
  • H2 (User Experience): Group class clients prefer to be guided by LEDs and to rank reaction times, because gamification distracts from exhaustion better than a traditional circuit. If false: the lights add no value over a wall timer.
  • H3 (Technical Viability): The ESP32 + tap sensors + accelerometer architecture withstands hundreds of daily impacts on walls, floors, and heavy bags without failing or losing connection over a month of commercial use. If false: the portable format is unviable, and we must go back to a fixed wired station.

Reformulated Hypotheses

  • H1 Reformulated: "We believe a HIIT/functional studio owner currently pays for some tool or process to direct their circuits (an extra instructor, a timer, an app) and recognizes an operational problem expensive enough to accept an institutional subscription of $X MXN/month above what an individual user would pay for a training app.
    If false: there is no value differential between 'for the studio' and 'for me', and the price must anchor to the consumer app range (60–150 MXN), forcing a low-ticket B2C model instead of B2B."
  • H2 Reformulated: "We believe adding visual stimuli and rankings to a reaction exercise increases motivation and the number of repetitions/touches compared with doing the same exercise without visual feedback, whether in a group, at home, or in a one-on-one session with a coach.
    If false: gamification doesn't change measurable behavior, and the product's value is reduced to a timer with lights."
  • H3: Remains unchanged.

New Hypothesis: Individual vs. Institutional Subscription Pricing

"We believe that if the product is sold as an individual subscription (not institutional), the price a gym user will accept is in the $79–149 MXN/month range, the same range they currently pay for Spotify, training streaming apps (like Peloton Digital or Adidas Training), or wearables with a premium app, and that a gym would be willing to subsidize or resell that subscription if the per-user price stays under that ceiling, or to pay a higher-priced subscription as long as the gym is the one offering the service.
If false: gym users don't value a reaction app at the same level as a streaming or general fitness app, and the real equilibrium price is lower (freemium with a hardware upsell), or higher only if the product is repositioned as a professional tool for the coach rather than the student."

Validation Synthesis (Questions and Answers)

  • 1. Which hypotheses did we test? H1 (would a studio owner pay a $1,500–2,000 MXN/month SaaS subscription?), H2 (do LEDs and rankings improve the experience in group classes?), and H3 (does the hardware withstand daily commercial use?), through 4 validation interviews.
  • 2. What did we find: confirmed, adjusted, or pivoted?
    H1 adjusted/pivoted: the only available price data point (60–100 MXN, like consumer apps) contradicts the $1,500–2,000 ticket, and none of the interviews was with the actual buyer (a HIIT studio owner).
    H2 partially adjusted: there is genuine interest in gamification, but no specific evidence for "group classes"; the interest showed up more for individual/home use.
    H3 untested: no interview could evaluate durability.
  • 3. The most important user quote: "It's free. Everything is free, there's no way to put an economic part to it." — the gym administrator, answering about the cost of not having a good reflex measurement solution.
  • 4. What changed in the concept (or why it stayed the same): The concept is adjusted: from "measuring reaction time" as the core value proposition to "making a circuit more fun and motivating," with measurement as part of the game rather than the main promise. The business model shifts from a high-ticket institutional SaaS toward hardware sales or a low-ticket individual subscription, until it is validated with the right buyer. The technology interaction (stimulus + touch + ranking) stays unchanged, because it still resonated in every interview.

  • HYPOTHESIS: The owner/administrator makes the purchase decision, and the coach influences it.
  • HYPOTHESIS: The member would pay an individual subscription, or the business would pay per location/device.

5. Critical Analysis

A. Where is the strongest value?
At the emotional level, specifically motivation and fun: these are the only values interviewees mentioned directly and repeatedly. The functional level shows that the problem (measuring reaction) is already solved in several ways (traffic light, Garmin, free tests, Kigo), which signals that measurement itself is not scarce. The transformational and social levels are, for now, interpretation.

B. What seems to be the real problem?
The interviews suggest that the problem is not a lack of measurement, but the chain measurement → frequent training → motivation → follow-up: the athlete with the Garmin has data but sometimes doesn't check it; the boxer receives measurement as part of an existing service (the coach), not as something bought separately. This points to the real challenge being sustaining the habit and engagement, not instrumenting the measurement.

C. Claims that are not yet proven:

  • Retention: 🔴 There is no evidence that rankings increase retention or attendance.
  • Churn: 🔴 No relevant dropout problem was identified in the interviews.
  • Coach savings: 🔴 Not mentioned by any interviewee.
  • Scalability: 🔴 Serving more members without hiring more staff is not validated.
  • ROI and willingness to pay: 🔴 No B2B data. The 60–100 MXN range is personal spending, not an institutional price.
  • Buying segment: 🟡 Unvalidated hypothesis (no interviewee was an owner/administrator of a HIIT studio).
  • Differentiation: 🔴 At least one competitor has been identified (Kigo), and differentiation from it has not been proven.

D. Which value proposition should be tested first?
The hypothesis "the business doesn't need more precision; it needs to turn measurement into a frequent, motivating, easy-to-follow training experience" has moderate support. It rests on the motivation/fun themes that came up and on the pattern of underused data (the Garmin case). But it is still a hypothesis: we lack direct evidence from a HIIT gym owner confirming this is their priority problem, and evidence that solving it creates willingness to pay.

E. What should the next commercial experiment test?
The experiment should aim directly at closing the gaps in section C, focusing on who buys and how much economic value they perceive, not only on validating the user experience. Stronger options than an opinion interview:

  1. A low-cost paid pilot (or a refundable deposit) with 2–3 real HIIT/CrossFit studios, to test whether the owner/administrator is willing to commit money.
  2. A letter of intent signed by the purchase decision-maker, conditional on the pilot's results.
  3. Testing two pricing models in parallel (per location vs. per device) to see which one reduces decision friction the most.

The goal of this experiment is not to confirm that people "like" the product, but to find out whether there is a real buyer willing to pay to solve the measurement → habit → motivation chain, and at what price.

5. Competitive Analysis & Blue Ocean Strategy

Part 1: Competitive Positioning Map

Segment: Independent coaches and administrators of sports/university facilities in Mexico, especially for combat sports, track, and conditioning.

Criterion: "Direct" means it competes with app-controlled interactive lights/panels for reaction training. "Indirect" solves the same need (improving reaction, agility, or circuit autonomy) with another technology. Prices are public international references; they do not necessarily include import costs, VAT, distribution, or service in Mexico.

Direct Competitors

  • BlazePod: A system of wireless light pods controlled by an app, with random stimuli, configurable exercises, real-time data, and competitive dynamics. Operates globally; available for international purchase. Kits from US$439, with free app plans and Pro memberships around US$12.99/month.
    Specific weakness: the total cost in Mexico, adding import and the Pro subscription, is far above the segment's software anchor of MX$60–100 per month; in addition, the coach must manage the app and place several pods before class, so it does not fully eliminate operational friction.
  • FITLIGHT: Wireless LED lights that turn off by touch or motion, with programmable exercises, results tracking, and individual or group use. Global, with direct shipping from the United States. System from US$999, with an app that has no subscription; each additional light is offered at around US$179.
    Specific weakness: it is designed and sold as professional/elite equipment, which makes it financially hard for a local gym or a Mexican university that prioritizes fun and competition over advanced neurocognitive analysis; shipping can also generate taxes and import costs, and the advertised warranty is one year.
  • Lummic: A system of four to eight reaction lights with visual and auditory stimuli, touch sensors, games, and a free app with more than 40 programs. Global, mainly Europe; no documented local operation in Mexico. Four-light kits from approximately €241.88, six from €349.69, and eight from €459; the app is permanently free.
    Specific weakness: its proposal is broad and playful, but public information shows a multi-segment orientation (sports, children, and older adults) rather than a solution focused on combat or track circuits with class flow, group rankings, and quick setup for several athletes.
  • ReactionX / VEGVISIR: Reaction pods with an app, touch, proximity, vibration, and sound sensors, battle modes, and an advertised precision of 0.01 seconds. Global; international distribution. Published packages from US$1,600 for four pods, although comparison distributors list some six-unit kits at roughly US$250–370, which suggests an inconsistent pricing/distribution structure.
    Specific weakness: the inconsistency between prices and channels makes institutional purchasing and budget comparison in Mexico difficult; there is also no localized offer for Mexican coaches who need support, local invoicing, Spanish content, and simple use during group classes.

Competitive reading: The first three are the benchmarks Lumetriq must beat in category perception. BlazePod is the closest rival because of its combination of hardware, app, randomness, and gamification; FITLIGHT dominates the professional perception; Lummic proves there is an alternative with a free app and less subscription friction.

Indirect Competitors

  • Quick Board: A pressure-sensitive physical board that provides real-time feedback to train reaction, speed, coordination, balance, and rehabilitation. Global, mainly the United States. Board from US$2,295–2,495, plus a software license of US$49.99 to US$199.99 per month depending on the number of athletes.
    Specific weakness: its fixed-board format concentrates training at one station and requires an investment and subscription incompatible with a Mexican gym that wants to move several athletes through a circuit; it is also geared more toward assessment, rehabilitation, and reporting than toward quick "first to touch wins" competition.
  • Reaction Light Trainer / phone reaction apps: Apps that turn available smartphones or tablets into light stimuli and record performance without buying dedicated hardware. Global; available on the App Store and Google Play. Generally free or with in-app purchases.
    Specific weakness: it lowers the initial investment, but it does not solve Lumetriq's physical bottleneck well: athletes still have to approach a small screen or depend on strategically placed phones; in a combat or track class, the devices can move, get damaged, or be distracting, and they do not offer a rugged touch surface.
  • QuickPLAY RXTR Pro Coach: A sports rebound panel with built-in visual lights and guided exercises, designed so the equipment "trains" the player without constant instructions from the coach. Global, mainly the UK/Europe. Around €129.99, with the option of connecting up to six reaction lights.
    Specific weakness: it focuses on passing, soccer, and rebound coordination, not on configurable spatial circuits for boxing, track, or institutional conditioning; in addition, a single panel limits the variety of paths and the simultaneous participation of a class.
  • Cones and agility equipment with signals from a partner: Cone circuits where the coach or a partner shouts numbers, points at a cone, or uses a light/signal to indicate the next movement. Mexico, LATAM, and global; a widely accessible practice. Basic cones from a few hundred pesos per set.
    Specific weakness: cones are cheap and flexible, but they keep exactly the problem Lumetriq aims to eliminate: the coach must decide and communicate each stimulus, control the order, watch who responded first, and reset the circuit.

Substitutes

  • Manual drill led by the coach (claps, whistle, shouting): The coach places cones, assigns zones, and gives an unpredictable auditory or verbal signal for the athlete to run, punch, block, or change direction. Low monetary cost (MX$100–500 for a whistle and cones); high operating cost (setup, voice, constant attention).
    Weakness against Lumetriq: it is the most deeply rooted substitute, but the coach becomes the system's "controller" and cannot simultaneously direct technique, safety, intensity, and individual correction.
  • Tennis balls, reaction balls, or thrown stimuli: A coach or athlete throws objects irregularly to force catches, dodges, and quick decisions. Low monetary cost (MX$50–300 per object); operating cost depends on a partner.
    Weakness against Lumetriq: it produces a more realistic and cheaper reaction, but it does not generate a reproducible sequence, an automatic score, a ranking, or a progress history; nor does it let a single coach keep several circuits running.
  • Interval timer or beep app: The coach uses a phone to generate intervals, sounds, or random signals while the athletes perform a previously explained pattern. Free or very low monetary cost.
    Weakness against Lumetriq: it automates timing, but not spatial direction, and it does not confirm who correctly deactivated the stimulus; the coach still has to call out the movement and settle disputes between athletes.

The Most Dangerous Competitor: The Manual Circuit with Cones and a Whistle

The most dangerous competitor is not BlazePod: it is the current behavior of using cones, a ball, a whistle, and the coach's voice. It has an almost zero marginal cost, it is already part of the training language, it works with any sport, and it requires no budget approval, connectivity, battery charging, or technology training. An app can replace part of the randomness, but that is not enough to displace a method the coach already knows how to improvise in seconds.

To get users to switch, Lumetriq would have to sell less "advanced measurement" and more class flow:

  • Set up a circuit in under a minute.
  • Let the coach pick a simple mode: "battle," "free-for-all," "defense," "change of direction," or "follow the light."
  • Trigger unpredictable stimuli without the coach having to shout instructions.
  • Automatically identify the correct touch and show who won each round.
  • Run small groups without each athlete needing an account.
  • Offer rankings, challenges, and basic progression without forcing an expensive B2B subscription.
  • Include Spanish content plus support, warranty, and invoicing suited to Mexico.
  • Keep an offline or low-internet-dependency experience.

Lumetriq's opportunity, therefore, is to position itself as a class assistant that frees up the coach, not as another sports assessment system. Its advantage would have to be: "the circuit runs itself, the athletes compete, and the coach goes back to focusing on correcting technique."

Pricing Implication

Lumetriq's price should not yet be set using only the cost of a MX$60–100 per month app as a reference. Direct competitors show that perceived value is concentrated mainly in the hardware, while the subscription can become a barrier: BlazePod charges for hardware plus a membership; FITLIGHT avoids the subscription but compensates with equipment costing approximately US$999; Lummic competes with a free app and lower-priced kits.

For this segment, the most coherent commercial hypothesis would be:

  • A one-time payment for the physical kit as the main offer.
  • A free base app with routines, competition, and essential progress tracking.
  • Optional paid features, only if the customer sees them as useful: multiple groups, advanced history, an institutional dashboard, results export, or multi-location management.
  • A small, expandable starter kit, so a coach doesn't have to buy an 8–12 unit system from the start.

That design would tackle both observable barriers at once: the rejection of expensive SaaS and the perception that imported systems are professional equipment outside a local gym's everyday budget.

Part 2: Blue Ocean

Concept: Lumetriq
Segment: Coaches and administrators of boutique, HIIT, CrossFit, and functional studios in Mexico, with high-intensity group classes, who currently direct their athletes' reaction training with manual methods (whistle, cones, timer) and refuse to pay for an expensive SaaS.

Four Actions Framework

ELIMINATE: the concept will not offer

  • Professional-level neurocognitive/sports analytics depth (scouting-style reports, clinical performance assessment). The segment's Layer 3 (psychographic) is explicit: they value motivation, variety, and competition ("first to touch wins") far above deep analysis. FITLIGHT already owns that professional/elite perception; competing there is a pure red ocean, and it also makes the product more expensive without the segment asking for it.

REDUCE: the concept will offer far below the industry standard

  • Minimum starter kit size required to begin: the industry standard pushes kits of 4 to 8+ units (BlazePod sells in packs of 6, ReactionX from 4, FITLIGHT from 2 but with expensive additional units). The segment's Layer 4 (economic) rejects a high entry commitment and anchors its real willingness to buy to an accessible one-time payment, not to a complete system from day one.

RAISE: the concept will offer far above the industry standard

  • Speed and autonomy of circuit setup (one-tap preset modes: "battle," "free-for-all," "change of direction"). The competitive map itself shows that neither BlazePod nor FITLIGHT removes the coach's operational friction: several pods have to be placed and the app managed before each class. Layer 2 (behavioral) confirms that the current method (claps, whistle) is already tedious; the goal is to free up the coach, not to give them another app to manage.
  • Full localization for Mexico (language, support, warranty, invoicing): none of the four documented direct competitors operates locally in Mexico; all are global operations with no confirmed local presence. The team has direct knowledge of the target user (real Mexican coaches and gyms, interviewed) as a declared differentiating capability.

CREATE: the concept will introduce for the first time

  • Multimodal impact measurement (force + acceleration/motion + proximity, not just binary reaction time). No competitor on the map measures punch intensity, only time/touch. It builds directly on the team's declared differentiating capability: integrated contact, force, accelerometer, and proximity sensors.
  • Persistent social progress across sessions (comparison with group peers over time, not just within a single class or "battle mode"). Following Hevy's validated pattern, where sustained social comparison is what keeps the habit going, no documented reaction competitor offers this as a core product feature; BlazePod only offers competition within a session, not an accumulated social history.

The blue ocean in one sentence:

Red Ocean Warning

The scores of 4 on attributes 1, 2, 3, 6, and 7 are intentional design, not validated evidence: none of those numbers comes from a working prototype or from an interview with the real buyer (a HIIT/boutique studio owner). If, when building the product, the multimodal sensors make the hardware more expensive, or if the circuit takes more than a minute to set up in practice, those scores fall toward BlazePod's 2, and Lumetriq ends up competing on the same dimensions as its closest rival, without the price cushion of the manual circuit or FITLIGHT's professional reputation. In addition, the "Actionable Segment" itself points to an unresolved, contradictory hypothesis: that gyms "might be willing to pay a higher subscription." If that turns out to be true, attribute 2 moves toward BlazePod's SaaS model, the very model the segment's Layer 4 (economic) says it rejects.

Key Question for the Team

Will the real owner of a boutique/HIIT/CrossFit studio in Mexico (not a university gym administrator or an individual coach) pay an accessible one-time price for the hardware, and keep using the free app long enough to generate the persistent social progress (attribute 8) that sustains the habit? Or does their real willingness to pay lie, as the unresolved hypothesis suggests, in a higher institutional subscription in exchange for managing an on-screen leaderboard for the whole class?
That answer decides whether Lumetriq looks more like the manual circuit with a technological leap, or ends up looking like BlazePod under another name.

Part 3: Value Proposition

Level in Bain's Pyramid

  • Current level: Functional
  • Justification: the identified pain (monotony in reaction drills) and gain (gamifying the stimulus with lights, sound, and rankings) are described in terms of what the product does and what it solves, not in terms of how it makes the coach or athlete feel about themselves.
  • Opportunity to move up: Yes, to Emotional, without exaggerating, because the segment's Layer 3 (psychographic) already opens the door: they value "competition" (first to touch wins) far above analytical data. That competitive drive and group pride are legitimate emotional material, not invented; moving up a level means naming that feeling, not promising life or identity transformation (that would skip a level without evidence).

IDEO Assessment

  • Insightful ✅: no direct competitor (BlazePod, FITLIGHT) combines unpredictable stimuli + persistent social progress at an accessible entry price; the blue ocean gap already made this clear.
  • Unique ⚠️: the real advantage (multimodal force/motion sensors) is hard to replicate, but the price of $1,500 per pod (a 4-pod package with the subscription included) has not yet been validated with the real buyer (a boutique/HIIT studio owner), so the uniqueness of "accessible" is a hypothesis, not a confirmed fact.
  • Targeted ✅: the segment has all 4 layers well defined (who, what they do today, what they value, how much they pay), precisely enough to speak to them directly without sounding generic.

Developing the Value Proposition

VERSION 1: FUNCTIONAL

"For HIIT, boxing, and functional coaches in Mexico: Lumetriq turns your reaction drills into a challenge with lights, sound, and rankings, without depending on a whistle or a timer."

  • Strength: it names the user, the result (a measurable challenge), and why not the current method (whistle/timer).
  • Limitation: it stays at describing the product's mechanics; it doesn't convey why the athlete would care about playing again next week.

VERSION 2: EMOTIONAL

"For HIIT, boxing, and functional coaches in Mexico: Lumetriq gets your students competing to be the fastest in the group, week after week, not just within one class."

  • Strength: it appeals directly to the "first to touch wins" that was already validated in the interviews; the hook is the pride of beating others, not just the exercise.
  • Risk: if "week after week" is not backed by a real, visible progress history in the app, it sounds like an empty promise; it depends on the persistent ranking actually being built as a core feature, not as decoration.

VERSION 3: THE STRONGEST

"For HIIT, boxing, and functional coaches in Mexico: Lumetriq turns every reaction drill into a challenge your students want to repeat, with a leaderboard that keeps the competition going week after week, not just on the day of class."

Why this is the strongest: it combines the functional (reaction drill, clear mechanics) with the emotional (wanting to repeat, competing across sessions) without losing either one. It also implicitly contrasts with the most dangerous competitor (the manual circuit generates no ranking or history) and with BlazePod (whose competition ends when the session ends), without naming them or sounding like a feature list.

6. AI-Generated Concept: Does It Look Rugged?

Short Answer: No

The images we got from Imagen 3 (Gemini) look good, but they show a consumer product, similar to a smart speaker. They don't look like gym equipment that takes punches from a boxer. The problem started with our prompt, not with the AI. This is what we asked for:

  • "soft, biomorphic pebble-like dome": a soft, rounded stone.
  • "transitions seamlessly without visible joints into a smooth, glossy hard plastic outer shell": glossy plastic with no joints.
  • "two-prong quick-release mount… similar to action camera accessories": the mount of an action camera.
  • "exposed concentric copper metal contact rings": copper contacts in plain sight.

Not a single word in the prompt mentioned loads, gloves, anchoring, or durability. The AI drew exactly what we asked for, and what we asked for can't take a punch.

"The AI casually draws things that would never survive a punch." A pretty image does not validate the engineering; what makes the pod rugged are the Week 5 requirements.

What Makes an Object Look Rugged

  • A shape with mass: a squat, heavy cylinder (Ø 160 × 75 mm, ≤ 1.6 kg), not a dome.
  • Matte, textured materials: stitched faux leather and matte plastic with signs of use, not gloss.
  • Visible hardware: it communicates that the pod is anchored and can be taken apart (NFR-06 from Week 5).
  • Fragile parts, protected: the light and the port stay away from the striking zone.
  • Human scale: a glove in the image shows the size and what it is for.
  • Gym references: heavy bag mounts, racks, and focus mitts, not speakers or camera accessories.

Corrected Prompt (v2)

This prompt translates the Week 5 requirements into visual language. It is written in English because the image generator interprets it better that way.

Photorealistic product photo of a rugged smart boxing reaction pod bolted flat to a bare concrete gym wall, at the height of a boxer's head. A red 16 oz boxing glove is hitting the center of the pad, slight motion blur, for scale.

Shape: a squat, heavy cylinder 160 mm in diameter and 75 mm deep. Not a dome, not a pebble.
Striking pad: 120 mm diameter, flat, black stitched vinyl leather over dense foam about 17 mm thick, like a boxing focus mitt. The pad sits inside a raised protective lip of the housing.
Housing: thick-walled matte charcoal polymer with a sandblasted texture, light scuffs and chalk marks from daily use.
Mounting: the pod sits flush against the wall on a steel back plate almost its full diameter, with visible bolt heads, so it is obviously anchored. No side bracket, no quick-release clip.
Light: a 120-degree arc of cyan LEDs recessed behind a thick frosted window in the housing lip, set back from the pad, glowing brightly.
Ports: one USB-C charging port with a rubber cover on the side. No open slots, no vents, no exposed metal contacts.
Style: industrial gym equipment, like heavy-bag mounts and squat-rack hardware. Even, realistic gym lighting.

Avoid: glossy plastic, biomorphic or pebble shapes, seamless shells, thin light strips on the striking edge, copper contacts, stacked units, small side brackets.

How to Evaluate the Next Render

  1. Where does a badly placed glove land? It must land on the pad or the lip, never on the light.
  2. How does the force travel to the wall? A direct path must be visible: pad, body, plate, and screws.
  3. What would break first? If the answer is "the light" or "the mount," the render is no good.
  4. Is the size clear? There must be a glove or a hand in the image.

Correct use of the image: the render is useful for communicating the product's intent. It is not useful for deciding the design. That decision is made with the requirements and their verification (PR-02: pendulum test with an inspection every 1,000 punches).

7. Reflection and Key Takeaways

This week taught us that validating is not the same as asking whether people like the idea. All four interviews showed interest in making training more fun, but none of them was with the person who actually pays, and the administrator's line, "it's free," made it clear that nobody pays to measure their reaction time today. The real problem is a different one: getting training to happen again and again, with follow-up and motivation. That is why our value proposition shifted from "measuring precisely" to "a challenge students want to repeat."

Sizing the market with TAM, SAM, and SOM pushed us to be transparent about where every number comes from. Most of the filters are assumptions, and the result (800 packages in two years, ≈ 267 gyms) is only profitable from year 1 because the team draws no salary, so the margin is thin. The competitive analysis showed us that our most dangerous rival is not BlazePod, but the cones and whistle the coach already uses for free. Plotting the Blue Ocean canvas made the advantage visible at a glance in a way the table alone never did: the one thing no competitor does is measure the impact of the punch, not just the time. In Week 5, that idea became a measurable requirement (FR-03) and a design decision: load cells under a floating plate.

We also learned to be suspicious of what AI makes look good. The concept images are attractive, but they describe a smart speaker rather than a device that takes punches, because the AI drew exactly what our prompt asked for. A rugged product doesn't come from adjectives; it comes from requirements that can be tested, like withstanding 10,000 punches of ≈ 1,500 N (PR-02) and mounting to the wall without a lever arm. Claude and Perplexity helped us structure the analysis, but we had to check every figure and every image against the sources, the interviews, and what the pod actually has to withstand.
